Last time there was a single connective. Implication turned out to be a function, and everything else followed from that. Now we add "and", "or" and "not".

Each of them will also turn out to be something familiar. And an asymmetry falls out along the way that is worth carrying with you: **a disjunction is cheap to prove and expensive to use, and a conjunction is exactly the other way round.**

## "And" is a pair

What does it take to prove “$A$ and $B$”? Obviously both proofs at once. So a proof of a conjunction is a **pair**, and you assemble it with angle brackets.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="and-intro" title="Assembling a conjunction" />

The same thing with tactics. `constructor` says: "the goal is composite, break it into parts" — and one goal becomes two.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="and-tactic" title="The same, with constructor" />

The dots before `exact` focus on one subgoal at a time. They are optional, but without them it is easy to lose track of which goal you are closing.

The other direction is easier still: from a pair you take either half.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="and-elim" title="Taking one half" />

Put the two directions together and you get commutativity — the whole proof fits on one line.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="and-comm" title="Order does not matter in a conjunction" />

## "Or" is a choice

Here it gets interesting. To prove “$A$ or $B$” one side is enough — but you have to **say which one**. Knowing that one of them holds is not enough; Lean wants it produced.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="or-intro" title="Pick a side" />

`Or.inl` and `Or.inr` are named after *left* and *right*. The tactics do the same job:

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="or-tactic" title="The left tactic" />

**Using** a disjunction is the hard direction. If you are handed “$A$ or $B$”, you do not know which half is true. So the proof has to go through in both cases — otherwise it is not a proof.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="or-elim" title="Case analysis" />

Read `cases h with | inl hA => ... | inr hB => ...` as: "if `h` came from the left, call it `hA` and prove the goal; if it came from the right, call it `hB` and prove the goal again."

<Concept type="idea" title="The symmetry worth noticing">
  "And" is expensive to prove and cheap to use: building the pair takes both halves, but pulling one out is a single move. "Or" mirrors it exactly: building is cheap, since one side is enough, and using is expensive, since you pay for both branches. This is not a quirk of Lean. It is an exact reflection of how the two connectives are built in ordinary mathematics.
</Concept>

## "Not" is not a connective at all

Negation in Lean is not a construction of its own. `¬A` is **shorthand** for `A → False`, that is, “$A$ implies a falsehood”.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="not-def" title="A definition, not a theorem" />

`Iff.rfl` works because there is nothing here to prove: both sides are the same expression, written two ways.

That tells you at once how to prove a negation. It is a function, so start with `intro`. You get a proof of $A$ and have to derive a contradiction from it.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="not-use" title="Nothing contradicts itself" />

Step through it. From `h : A ∧ ¬A` we get `h.1 : A` and `h.2 : ¬A`. But `h.2` is a function from `A` to `False`. Apply it to `h.1` and out comes `False` — exactly what was required.

## Anything follows from a falsehood

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch02/Connectives.lean" region="contradiction" title="False.elim closes any goal" />

It looks like cheating, but it is an honest rule: **if the assumptions contradict each other, there is nothing left to prove**. The statement “if $A$ and not $A$, then $B$” is true simply because its hypothesis can never be met.

<Concept type="mistake" title="This does not mean you can prove anything">
  Everything follows from `False` — but `False` itself cannot be proved. The rule only fires when a contradiction is already sitting in the context, that is, when someone has handed you mutually exclusive assumptions. In a consistent system you never get as far as `False.elim`.
</Concept>

## Putting it together

De Morgan's law: if “$A$ or $B$” is false, then both are false. All three techniques show up at once — `constructor` for the conjunction in the goal, `intro` for each negation, a choice of side to build the contradiction.

## Practice

1. Prove `theorem my_and_swap (A B : Prop) (h : A ∧ B) : B ∧ A` twice: as a one-liner and with tactics.
2. Prove `theorem or_idem (A : Prop) (h : A ∨ A) : A`.
3. Prove `theorem and_imp (A B C : Prop) (h : A ∧ B → C) : A → B → C`.
4. Prove `theorem contrapositive (A B : Prop) (h : A → B) : ¬B → ¬A`.
5. Prove `theorem and_or_distrib (A B C : Prop) (h : A ∧ (B ∨ C)) : (A ∧ B) ∨ (A ∧ C)`.
6. Try to prove the converse De Morgan law: `¬(A ∧ B) → ¬A ∨ ¬B`. It will not go through — find out exactly where you get stuck.

<details>
  <summary>Answers and notes</summary>

  1. `⟨h.2, h.1⟩`, or `by constructor; · exact h.2; · exact h.1`.
  2. `by cases h with | inl hA => exact hA | inr hA => exact hA` — both branches are identical, and you still have to walk both.
  3. `by intro hA hB; exact h ⟨hA, hB⟩` — the pair is assembled right inside the argument.
  4. `by intro hnB hA; exact hnB (h hA)` — given `A`, turn it into `B` and run that into `¬B`.
  5. `by cases h.2 with | inl hB => exact Or.inl ⟨h.1, hB⟩ | inr hC => exact Or.inr ⟨h.1, hC⟩` — split on the **second half** of the pair and carry the first half along. It is in the chapter file as `and_or_distrib`.
  6. It does not go through, and that is not a gap in your technique. Start as usual: `intro h` gives you `h : ¬(A ∧ B)` and the goal `¬A ∨ ¬B`. The goal is a disjunction, so the very next step is to pick a side — and that is where it dies. Take `left` and you owe a proof of `¬A`; all you know is that `A` and `B` do not both hold, which says nothing about `A` on its own. Take `right` and you are stuck in the mirror image. To choose you would first have to know whether `A` is true, and the principle that hands you that is the law of excluded middle: `A ∨ ¬A` for an arbitrary `A`. It is a classical extra, not something the constructive machinery of this chapter produces. Classically the statement is true, and Lean does have excluded middle available (`Classical.em`, and the `by_cases` tactic); we pick it up when we get to proof by contradiction.

</details>

## Summary

- `A ∧ B` is a pair: built with `⟨_, _⟩` or `constructor`, taken apart with `.1` and `.2`.
- `A ∨ B` is a choice: proved with `Or.inl`/`Or.inr` or the `left`/`right` tactics, and used only through case analysis with `cases`.
- A disjunction is cheap to prove and expensive to use; a conjunction is the other way round.
- `¬A` is `A → False`, so a negation is proved by `intro`.
- Anything follows from a contradiction, but you only reach a contradiction when the assumptions are mutually exclusive.

Next come the quantifiers: "for all" will turn out to be a familiar function, and "there exists" will demand an actual example.

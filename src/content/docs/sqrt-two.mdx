The last one. Irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ is the proof people usually formalise first, and for good reason: it is short, everyone knows it, and unlike most short arguments it is made of genuine steps rather than a computation.

Along the way there is a technique worth carrying off with you: **the division of labour between tactics**. Everything non-linear goes to `ring`, and then `omega` works with $m^2$ and $k^2$ as if they were ordinary unknowns. Hardly anything is left to do by hand.

## The parity lemma

The school proof leans on something that is normally said in passing: if $m^2$ is even then $m$ is even. Here it has to be said in full.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch09/SqrtTwo.lean" region="parity-lemma" title="An even square comes from an even number" />

Case analysis: a number is either even or odd. In the first case there is nothing to prove. In the second we write $m = 2k+1$, expand the square with `ring` and land on $2(\ldots)+1$ — odd beyond argument, which contradicts the hypothesis.

Look at how the roles are divided. `ring` does the one non-linear thing in sight: it turns $(2k+1)^2$ into $2(2k^2+2k)+1$. Everything after that is `omega`'s.

<Concept type="remember" title="What omega actually sees">
  `omega` decides **linear** arithmetic over `Nat` and `Int`: addition, subtraction, multiplication by numerals, `/` and `%` by numerals, comparisons. It knows nothing about `^`. What saves the day is that it does not choke on what it does not know — an unrecognised term like `k ^ 2` simply becomes an opaque unknown, one atom with no internal structure.

  So the pattern is: use `ring` (or `nlinarith`, or a `calc` block) to bring the statement into a shape that is **linear in whatever compound terms remain**, then let `omega` finish. It works far beyond this chapter, and it is usually cheaper than doing the arithmetic by hand.
</Concept>

## The main statement

Now the proof itself, stated the way school states it: there is no fraction in lowest terms whose square is two.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch09/SqrtTwo.lean" region="no-fraction" title="No fraction in lowest terms squares to two" />

Walk through it — the steps are the ones you already know.

1. Suppose the opposite: $m^2 = 2n^2$.
2. Then $m^2$ is even, so by the lemma $m$ is even too. Write $m = 2k$.
3. Substitute: $4k^2 = 2n^2$, cancel the two, and you get $n^2 = 2k^2$.
4. Now it is $n^2$ that is even, and therefore $n$.
5. Both numbers are divisible by two, so two divides their greatest common divisor. But for a fraction in lowest terms that divisor is one. Contradiction.

Notice that `omega` is doing the same trick three times over. In `hn_sq` it holds two facts — $m^2 = 2n^2$ and $m^2 = 4k^2$ — and three atoms it cannot look inside, `m ^ 2`, `n ^ 2` and `k ^ 2`. That is enough: as a linear problem in those three unknowns it is trivial. The last `omega` is even blunter — from `2 ∣ 1` anything follows.

<Concept type="idea" title="What formalising buys you">
  On paper step 3 is written "cancel the two" and you move on. Here it had to be said out loud, and it turned out to be two things: first `ring` turns $(2k)^2$ into $4k^2$, and only then does `omega` conclude that $n^2 = 2k^2$. Neither part is hard — but until you write it down you do not notice that there are two of them and not one.
</Concept>

<Concept type="mistake" title="A condition that was not needed">
  The school statement usually carries "where $n \ne 0$" — a denominator cannot be zero, after all. Formalising showed that the condition is not needed. The only number coprime to zero is one, so $n = 0$ forces $m = 1$, and that case falls over by itself. The linter noticed an unused hypothesis, and it came out of the statement.

  Worth making a habit of. If a condition never got used, look at it again: either the proof is quietly missing something, or the statement was carrying dead weight.
</Concept>

## Practice

1. Prove `even_of_even_sq` yourself, without looking above.
2. Prove that if $m^2$ is divisible by three then so is $m$. How does this proof differ from the one for two?
3. Prove `example : Irrational (Real.sqrt 5)` using Mathlib.
4. Explain why the same argument does not go through for $\sqrt{4}$ — at which step does it break?
5. Prove `example : Real.sqrt 9 = 3`.

<details>
  <summary>Answers and notes</summary>

  1. Same shape: `rcases Nat.even_or_odd m`, then in the odd case `ring` expands the square and `omega` finishes the job.
  2. For three the case analysis is longer: three remainders instead of two ($3k$, $3k+1$, $3k+2$), and each square has to be worked out. Same idea, more work — which is exactly why Mathlib states the theorem once, for an arbitrary prime.
  3. `(by norm_num : Nat.Prime 5).irrational_sqrt`.
  4. It breaks at the very first step. For four the claim "if $m^2$ is divisible by 4 then $m$ is divisible by 4" is simply false: $m = 2$ gives $m^2 = 4$. That is why the theorem is stated for **primes**. For composite numbers that transition fails, and it is not an incidental detail — it is the whole engine of the proof.
  5. `by rw [show (9 : ℝ) = 3 ^ 2 by norm_num, Real.sqrt_sq (by norm_num : (0:ℝ) ≤ 3)]`.

</details>

## Summary

- A proof by contradiction in Lean looks exactly like the paper one: `intro` on the negation of the goal, then work towards `False`.
- Auxiliary lemmas have to be named and proved separately — "obviously" is not available here.
- A good division of labour: `ring` takes the non-linear rewriting, `omega` draws the linear conclusions about whatever came out of it.
- `omega` treats terms it does not understand as atoms. That is a feature, and it is what makes the pattern above work.
- An unused hypothesis is a reason to check whether the statement needs it at all.
- A teaching proof and working code are different things: Mathlib has the fact already, and on a real problem you take it.

## rw: substitute and move on

The `rw` tactic takes an equality and replaces every occurrence of its left-hand side in the goal with the right-hand side.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch04/Rewriting.lean" region="rw-basic" title="One substitution and the goal is gone" />

Look at what happened after the substitution: the goal became `b + 1 = b + 1`, and `rw` closed it on its own. That happens a lot — `rw` tries `rfl` after every rewrite.

You can hand it several equalities at once; they are applied in order.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch04/Rewriting.lean" region="rw-closes" title="A chain of substitutions" />

Sometimes you want the opposite — replace the right-hand side by the left. That is what the left arrow is for.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch04/Rewriting.lean" region="rw-direction" title="Rewriting backwards" />

<Concept type="mistake" title="rw changes the goal, not a hypothesis">
  By default `rw [h]` acts on the goal. To rewrite inside a hypothesis you have to say so: `rw [h] at hyp`. The standard beginner's mistake is to stare at an unchanged hypothesis and wonder why nothing happened.
</Concept>

## calc: a derivation as you would write it on paper

A long chain of `rw` reads badly: you can see what was done, but not what things looked like along the way. That is what `calc` is for — it lays the derivation out exactly as you would write it by hand, and demands a justification for every step.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch04/Rewriting.lean" region="calc-chain" title="A chain of equalities" />

The underscore means "whatever came out of the previous line". Read it top to bottom like any derivation on paper, while Lean checks that each step really does follow from what is written after `:=`.

## ring: routine work to the machine

Expanding brackets and collecting like terms can be done by hand, but it is precisely the work a human has no business doing. The `ring` tactic proves any identity that holds in every commutative ring — and that covers all of school algebra with $+$, $-$, $\cdot$ and natural-number powers.

## Where the line runs

Here is the distinction worth nailing down straight away.

An **identity** holds for all values of the variables: $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$. There is nothing to check and nothing to solve — `ring` handles it.

An **equation** is a condition on an unknown: $x + 3 = 10$. It does not hold always, only for one particular $x$. Here `ring` is useless: it cannot use hypotheses at all, it only reduces both sides of the goal to a common form.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch04/Rewriting.lean" region="ring-limits" title="This one needs linarith, not ring" />

`linarith` is a solver for linear problems: it looks at the hypotheses in the context and derives the goal from them. There is more about it in the chapter on inequalities.

<Concept type="idea" title="Why the distinction matters">
  School writes both with the same equals sign and tells them apart by context: "simplify" versus "solve". Lean makes you name the difference out loud. An identity is a statement with a "for all" in front of it; an equation is a statement about one particular unknown, under given hypotheses. Different statements, so different tactics.
</Concept>

## Practice

1. Prove `theorem sub_self_eq (a : ℤ) : a - a = 0`.
2. Prove `theorem expand_prod (a b : ℤ) : (a + 1) * (b + 1) = a * b + a + b + 1`.
3. Prove `theorem cube_of_sum (a b : ℤ) : (a + b) ^ 3 = a ^ 3 + 3 * a ^ 2 * b + 3 * a * b ^ 2 + b ^ 3`.
4. Prove `theorem use_hyp (x y : ℤ) (h : x = y + 2) : x - y = 2`.
5. Write a `calc` chain proving `(a - b) ^ 2 = a ^ 2 - 2 * a * b + b ^ 2`, in at least three steps.
6. Try to prove `theorem not_identity (a b : ℤ) : (a + b) ^ 2 = a ^ 2 + b ^ 2` with `ring`. What does it say, and why is that the right answer?

<details>
  <summary>Answers and notes</summary>

  1. `by ring`.
  2. `by ring`.
  3. `by ring` — this one is in the chapter file as `cube_of_sum`.
  4. `by rw [h]; ring`, or simply `by linarith` — either way the hypothesis has to be put to work.
  5. For instance: `calc (a - b) ^ 2 = (a - b) * (a - b) := by ring`, then `_ = a * a - a * b - b * a + b * b := by ring`, then `_ = a ^ 2 - 2 * a * b + b ^ 2 := by ring`.
  6. `ring` will not prove it, and rightly so: the statement is false. The two sides differ by $2ab$, which vanishes only when $a = 0$ or $b = 0$ — so this is no identity. The tactic reports that it reduced both sides to normal form and the two forms did not match.

</details>

## Summary

- `rw [h]` substitutes an equality into the goal, `rw [← h]` substitutes the other way round, `rw [h] at hyp` works inside a hypothesis.
- `calc` lays a derivation out as a chain and demands a justification for every step; it reads like handwriting.
- `ring` proves identities built from $+$, $-$, $\cdot$ and powers — the whole of school bracket-expanding.
- An identity and an equation are different animals: `ring` ignores hypotheses, so equations need other tactics.

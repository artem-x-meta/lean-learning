Equalities are settled, and so are numbers. What is left is the inequality — the thing a human proves noticeably worse than a machine does, because a case is so easy to drop.

The idea of this chapter: **a solver can be told things, and should be**. It is not all-powerful. But hand it one right fact and it finishes the job alone.

## linarith: adding hypotheses up

`linarith` looks for a linear combination of the hypotheses that gives the goal. That sounds abstract, but in practice it is exactly what you do by hand: add inequalities together and multiply them by positive numbers.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch07/Inequalities.lean" region="linarith-basic" title="From hypotheses to the goal" />

Systems work too. Here the tactic worked out the coefficients on its own:

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch07/Inequalities.lean" region="linarith-combination" title="A system of linear equations" />

Add the two equations and you get $3x = 9$. That is the combination `linarith` found — you never had to say which multiples to take, only that the answer follows.

## Where linearity ends

As soon as a variable is squared, `linarith` is helpless: it cannot multiply unknowns by each other. It sees `x ^ 2` as an atom, an opaque thing it knows nothing about. That the atom happens to be a square, and therefore never negative, is invisible to it.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch07/Inequalities.lean" region="linarith-fails" title="A square is not linear any more" />

`positivity` is the narrow specialist for this: it proves an expression positive or non-negative by taking its structure apart. Squares, sums of squares, absolute values — its territory. It never looks at the hypotheses at all, so either the shape of the expression settles the matter or the tactic gives up.

## nlinarith and the power of a hint

`nlinarith` is `linarith` that first throws in a few products and squares of its own. Sometimes that is enough. More often you have to **name the key fact**, and that is what the square brackets are for.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch07/Inequalities.lean" region="nlinarith" title="Two squares against twice the product" />

Take apart what happened. We handed the tactic one fact, `sq_nonneg (a - b)` — that is, $(a-b)^2 \ge 0$. Expand the bracket and it reads $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \ge 0$; the goal is that with one term moved across. All the content sits in the choice of hint; the rest is mechanics.

<Concept type="idea" title="The hint is the mathematics">
  Notice what we actually did. We did not prove the inequality, and we did not hand the whole thing to the machine either — we **named the idea of the proof** in one line. "Consider the square of the difference" is exactly what a person says at the blackboard. What follows is manipulation, and manipulation is worth delegating.
</Concept>

The same trick on a different inequality, and the hint does not change:

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch07/Inequalities.lean" region="nlinarith-hint" title="The same hint, a different goal" />

This is the mean inequality in one of its shapes: a product never beats the average of the two squares. Different statement, same one-line idea.

And completing the square, familiar from school:

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch07/Inequalities.lean" region="transitivity-chain" title="x² − 4x + 5 ≥ 1" />

Here $x^2 - 4x + 5 = (x-2)^2 + 1$, so the hint is the square of $x - 2$ — of the expression that drives the trinomial to its minimum. The hint always has that same shape; only what goes inside the bracket changes.

<Concept type="mistake" title="A hypothesis you never used">
  When writing down the conditions of a problem it is easy to add too much: "let $a \ge 0$ and $b \ge 0$". Lean's linter will point it out — it notices unused hypotheses. Listen to it: often it means the statement holds more generally than you assumed. $ab \le \frac{a^2+b^2}{2}$ is exactly such a case — it holds for all real numbers, not only the non-negative ones.
</Concept>

## Practice

Work in the playground: open any snippet above, clear it and write your own.

1. Prove `example (x : ℝ) (h : x ≥ 5) : 2 * x ≥ 10`.
2. Prove `example (a b : ℝ) (h1 : a + b = 10) (h2 : a - b = 4) : a = 7`.
3. Prove `theorem sum_of_squares_nonneg (a b : ℝ) : a ^ 2 + b ^ 2 ≥ 0`.
4. Prove `example (x : ℝ) : x ^ 2 + 1 > 0`.
5. Prove `example (a b : ℝ) : (a + b) ^ 2 ≤ 2 * (a ^ 2 + b ^ 2)` — the hint is the one from the chapter.
6. Prove `example (x : ℝ) : x ^ 2 - 6 * x + 10 > 0`. Which square do you hint?

<details>
  <summary>Answers and notes</summary>

  1. `by linarith`.
  2. `by linarith` — the tactic adds the two equations up itself.
  3. `by positivity`.
  4. `by positivity` — a square plus a positive number.
  5. `by nlinarith [sq_nonneg (a - b)]`. The difference between the two sides is exactly $(a-b)^2$. Once the square has been named nothing non-linear is left to do, and plain `linarith [sq_nonneg (a - b)]` closes it as well — worth trying, because it shows how little the solver had to do after the hint.
  6. `by nlinarith [sq_nonneg (x - 3)]` — complete the square: $x^2 - 6x + 10 = (x-3)^2 + 1$. The hint keeps its shape: the square of whatever expression sends the trinomial to its minimum.

</details>

## Summary

- `linarith` finds a linear combination of the hypotheses that gives the goal — the machine version of "add the inequalities up".
- Multiplying two variables takes the problem across the border of linearity.
- `nlinarith [hint]` reaches the non-linear cases once you supply the key fact; choosing that fact is the part that is actually mathematics.
- `positivity` proves non-negativity from the structure of the expression, and ignores hypotheses entirely.
- An unused hypothesis is a signal: the statement probably holds more generally than you stated it.

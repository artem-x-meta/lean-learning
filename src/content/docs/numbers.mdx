So far we have proved everything by hand. But a large share of statements about numbers is too dull to be worth a human: check a remainder, compare two expressions, read a value off a linear condition. For those there are **solvers** — tactics that finish the job by themselves.

They are useful exactly as far as you understand their limits. So here are three, and the place where each one breaks.

  'check concrete numeric statements with decide',

## decide: just compute

If a statement is **decidable** — that is, its truth can be settled by a finite computation — then `decide` performs that computation.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch05/Numbers.lean" region="decide-basic" title="Settled by computing" />

The second line uses `∣`, the divisibility sign; it gets a section of its own below.

The limitation follows straight from the definition. A statement about an arbitrary $n$ is not decidable: there are infinitely many numbers, and the search never ends. So `decide` only works where everything is concrete.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch05/Numbers.lean" region="decide-limits" title="Concrete values only" />

<Concept type="mistake" title="decide can stall on a true statement">
  Decidable does not mean quick. Sometimes the check needs a search so large that Lean runs into its limits and gives up — even though the statement is true and checkable in principle. If `decide` is thinking suspiciously long, that is not a reason to wait: most likely you want a different tactic.
</Concept>

## omega: linear problems with variables

Here is the tactic that lifts the main restriction. `omega` works with **variables and hypotheses** — but only while everything stays linear: addition, subtraction, multiplication by a constant, comparisons, remainders.

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch05/Numbers.lean" region="omega" title="Variables and hypotheses" />

Parity and remainders are linear as well, so `omega` takes such statements whole:

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch05/Numbers.lean" region="omega-parity" title="A sum of evens is even" />

Notice what has been proved: a statement about **any** $a$ and $b$, not about particular ones. That is a different level from `decide`.

<Concept type="idea" title="How to choose between the three">
  Ask yourself: **are there variables in the statement?** No — take `decide` or `norm_num` (the second is cleverer). Yes — then ask whether the problem is linear. If it is, `omega`. If it is not, you are proving it yourself or looking for a more specialised tactic. Multiplying two variables makes the problem nonlinear, and `omega` refuses on the spot.
</Concept>

## Divisibility is existence

The sign `∣` in `a ∣ b` is not the vertical bar on your keyboard but a separate symbol (in the editor you type `\dvd`). What it means is exactly the school definition: there exists a $c$ such that $b = a \cdot c$.

Since it is an existence claim, it is proved by producing a witness — the same move as in the quantifiers chapter:

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch05/Numbers.lean" region="divides" title="Three divides twelve" />

And transitivity of divisibility is just substituting one witness into the other:

<LeanSnippet file="DiveLean/Ch05/Numbers.lean" region="divides-trans" title="Divisibility is transitive" />

Read it like this: if $b = a \cdot x$ and $c = b \cdot y$, then $c = a \cdot (x \cdot y)$. The witness for the conclusion is the product of the two you were handed.

## Practice

1. Prove `example : (123 : Nat) % 10 = 3`.
2. Prove `example : Nat.Prime 101`.
3. Prove `example : (3 : ℚ) / 6 = 1 / 2`.
4. Prove `theorem odd_plus_odd (a b : Nat) (ha : a % 2 = 1) (hb : b % 2 = 1) : (a + b) % 2 = 0`.
5. Prove `theorem five_divides_twenty : (5 : Nat) ∣ 20`.
6. Try to prove `theorem square_nonneg (n : Int) : n * n ≥ 0` with `omega`. Why does it not go through?

<details>
  <summary>Answers and notes</summary>

  1. `by decide` — or `by norm_num`.
  2. `by norm_num` — primality is beyond what plain `decide` can manage.
  3. `by norm_num`.
  4. `by omega` — this example is in the chapter file as `odd_plus_odd`.
  5. `⟨4, rfl⟩` — the witness is four. Or `by decide`.
  6. `omega` refuses: `n * n` is a product of two variables, and that is nonlinear. The statement is true, but it needs a different instrument; in the chapter on inequalities that instrument will be `nlinarith`, which is built to handle squares.

</details>

## Summary

- `decide` settles decidable statements by computing — only about concrete values, and only while the search does not blow up.
- `norm_num` is arithmetic in earnest: fractions, comparisons, primality.
- `omega` works with variables and hypotheses while the problem is linear; multiplying variables puts it out of reach.
- `a ∣ b` says a factor exists, so it is proved by producing a witness.

Next comes induction: the only way to prove something about all natural numbers at once without checking them one by one.
